Essay · Fortress Malta · Part 3

Keith Park and the air defence of the island

The man who ran the Battle of Britain’s hardest sector arrived on Malta in July 1942, and within a fortnight had turned a defence into an offence.

James Holland · Friday 19 February 2027 · 12 min read · 23 commentsArchive · members
Banner image · 21:9
Park at Takali, August 1942 · Imperial War Museum / placeholder credit

Hugh Lloyd had held Malta through the worst of it, and he was exhausted. His replacement stepped off a Sunderland at Kalafrana on the morning of 14 July 1942 with a reputation, a plan, and no patience at all for the way the island had been fighting.

Park had spent 1940 commanding 11 Group, the sector that took the weight of the Luftwaffe’s assault on south-east England. He understood, better than anyone alive, how to use a small force of fighters against a larger attacking one, and he could see at once that Malta was doing it wrong.

“Why are we waiting for them to arrive? Meet them over the sea, before they reach the island.”

The Forward Interception Plan, as it became known, was simple to state and hard to do. Rather than scramble when the raiders were overhead, the Spitfires would climb to meet them north of the island, over the water, and break the bomber formations before they could line up on the airfields.

Photograph · 3:2
Spitfires at Luqa · placeholder credit

Within a week the raids on the airfields had fallen away. Within a month the Luftwaffe had largely given up daylight attacks on Malta altogether. This is the part of the story that the George Cross citation does not tell, and it is the part that decided the siege.

There is a further question, which Park himself never answered in public, about what he had been told before he arrived. The signals traffic between Cairo and London in June 1942 suggests the decision to send him had been taken weeks earlier, and that the Forward Interception Plan was not improvised on the island but carried there.
